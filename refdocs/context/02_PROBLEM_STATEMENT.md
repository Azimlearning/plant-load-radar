## As received (DXP brief page, transcribed)

### Problem overview

A building materials plant produces AAC blocks, precast, or ready-mix and serves two types of customers: external contractors and developers who buy at market terms, and the company's own construction and property businesses.

When plant capacity is tight, the plant has to choose which customers to serve. The plant manager is typically focused on volume and margin, while the wider group may prioritise internal projects where a supply delay can affect project schedules and programme days. Both positions are reasonable, but there is currently no clear or consistent way to make this decision. As a result, capacity is allocated informally, deal by deal.

A second problem is demand planning. Demand is forecast independently at each plant using a mix of spreadsheets, judgement, and managers' knowledge. This can result in excess inventory in some situations and missed orders in others. There is no single view of committed demand across the group's internal project pipeline and external customer orders.

### Your mission

How might you improve capacity allocation and demand planning so that the plant and the wider group can make better commercial and operational decisions?

You should:
- Propose how capacity allocation decisions should be made and who should make them when capacity is constrained.
- Quantify what the current arrangement costs the business, such as working capital, lost margin, or project programme days, and show your working.
- Demonstrate how demand from internal projects and external customers could be viewed together to support better decisions.
- Identify where AI genuinely helps in an environment where plant-level information is largely stored in paper records, WhatsApp, and spreadsheets — and where using AI may be premature.
- Build the component or solution you propose and bring it working, as something a plant scheduler or plant supervisor could actually use in their day-to-day work.

**Constraints:** No new ERP, no consultant, and no transfer-pricing policy simply handed down by head office. The challenge must be addressed within 12 weeks and cannot be solved simply by escalating the decision to someone senior enough to impose a rule.

**How do you know it worked?** Success should be measured through a real business outcome — a change in behaviour or cash movement, such as improved inventory, margin, working capital, or avoided programme delays. Not dashboard logins, satisfaction scores, or process adoption.
